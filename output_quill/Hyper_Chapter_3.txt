# CHAPTER 3: THE HANGOVER

The dream was always the same.
Erin was standing in a courtroom. But it wasn't a normal courtroom. The walls were made of black, dripping tar. The judge's bench was a towering throne of bone.
The defendant was herself.
She was naked, shivering in the cold.
The prosecutor was James Van Hopper. But his eyes were gone. Just empty sockets weeping black oil.
"She knew," Van Hopper whispered. His voice came from everywhere at once. "She knew what I was. And she let me go."
The jury laughed. It was the sound of breaking glass.
Erin looked at the jury box.
It was filled with corpses.
Mrs. Van Hopper. The boy she had defended in the drunk driving case. The girl from the arson trial.
They were all looking at her.
"Guilty," they chanted. "Guilty. Guilty."
The floor opened up beneath her.
She fell.
Down.
Down.
Into the dark.

Erin woke up with a gasp, sitting bolt upright in bed.
Her heart was hammering against her ribs like a trapped bird. *Thump-thump-thump.*
She looked around the room.
Chrome. Glass. White sheets.
Her condo.
She was safe.
She looked at the clock.
6:00 AM.
The alarm hadn't even gone off yet.

She swung her legs out of bed. Her head throbbed—a dull, rhythmic ache behind her left eye. The vodka tonic.
She walked to the bathroom, her feet sinking into the deep pile carpet. It felt like walking on moss.
She turned on the tap. Cold water.
She splashed her face, gasping at the shock.
She looked at herself in the mirror.
Thirty-six years old. Partner track. Successful.
And terrified of her own dreams.

She started her routine. It was a ritual. A way to impose order on chaos.
Shower. Hot. Scrub the skin until it's pink. Wash away the dream.
Brush teeth. Two minutes exactly.
Floss.
Hair. Pulled back. Tight. No stray strands. Control.
Makeup. Foundation to hide the circles under her eyes. Mascara to make them look sharp. Lipstick. "Power Red."

She walked to the kitchen.
It was a masterpiece of modern design. Stainless steel appliances that cost more than her first car. Granite countertops.
And silence.
"Jasper?" she called out.
The Persian cat usually ran to her in the morning, demanding food.
Today, he was silent.
Erin frowned.
"Jasper?"
She found him in the living room.
He was standing in the center of the rug.
He wasn't moving.
He was staring at the front door.
His tail was twitching. *Swish. Swish.*
"What is it, buddy?" Erin asked, kneeling down. "You see a bug?"
Jasper didn't look at her. He hissed.
A low, guttural sound that didn't sound like a cat at all.
Erin looked at the door.
It was locked. The deadbolt was thrown. The chain was on.
But the air in the room felt... strange.
Charged.
Like the air before a thunderstorm. The hair on Erin's arms stood up.
Static electricity?
She walked to the door and checked the peephole.
Nothing. Just the empty hallway.
"You're being paranoid," she whispered to herself. "It's the hangover. It's the dream."

She went back to the kitchen and made coffee. Black. Strong.
She stood by the floor-to-ceiling window, looking out at the city.
Chicago was waking up. A grey beast stretching its limbs. Rain slicked the streets below, turning the headlights of cars into streaks of blood and gold.
She took a sip of coffee.
It tasted... metallic.
She frowned. She looked at the cup.
It was fine.
She took another sip.
Copper. It tasted like copper. Like old pennies. Or blood.
She poured the coffee into the sink.
"Filter must be bad," she muttered.

She grabbed her briefcase. She checked the locks one last time.
She looked at Jasper. He was still staring at the door.
"Bye, Jasper. Be good."
She opened the door and stepped out into the hall.
She locked it behind her.
As she walked to the elevator, she heard a sound from inside her apartment.
*Thump.*
A heavy, dull sound.
Like a body hitting the floor.
Erin froze.
She put her ear to the door.
Silence.
"Jasper?"
Nothing.
Maybe he jumped off the counter.
Yeah. That was it.
She pressed the elevator button.
But as the doors closed, she couldn't shake the feeling that she had left something behind.
Or that something had been waiting for her to leave.
something that was now inside, alone with the cat.

# CHAPTER 4: THE SUMMIT

Karl Gunderson’s office was designed to make you feel small.
It was located on the 40th floor, the "Partner's Skydeck." The ceilings were twenty feet high. The windows were vast sheets of glass that offered a view of the lake, the city, and the curvature of the earth.
Karl sat behind a desk that looked like it had been carved from the hull of a Viking ship.
He was reading a file.
He didn't look up when Erin entered.
"Sit," he said.

Erin sat in one of the leather chairs. They were low, forcing her to look up at him. A classic power move.
"You look like hell," Karl said, finally looking up.
"Bad dreams," Erin said. "And the coffee tasted like pennies."
"Copper piping. Building is getting old." Karl closed the file. "But you're here. Early. Good."

He slid the file across the desk.
"Father Richard Moore."
Erin picked it up. It was thin. Surprisingly thin for a homicide.
"Tell me," she said.
"Negligent homicide," Karl said, leaning back. "The victim was Emily Rose. Nineteen years old. Student at the university. She died two weeks ago."
"Died of what?"
"Malnutrition. Dehydration. Exhaustion. The Medical Examiner says she starved to death."
"And the priest?"
"The priest was conducting an exorcism. He convinced her she was possessed. He told her to stop taking her medication for epilepsy. He told her to fast."

Erin opened the file. She saw the photo of Emily Rose.
The "Before" photo. Smiling. Braces. Hopeful.
Then the "After" photo.
Erin's stomach turned.
The girl in the photo looked like a concentration camp victim. Her eyes were sunken black pits. Her skin was bruised, lacerated. Her mouth was a rictus of terror.
"My god," Erin whispered.
"The DA is Ethan Thomas," Karl said.
Erin looked up sharply. "Thomas? The crusader?"
"The very one. He's treating this as a holy war. He wants to prove that religious superstition killed this girl. He wants to put the Church on trial."

"And the Church wants me to stop him."
"The Church wants you to get an acquittal. Or at least a mistrial. They don't care about the theology, Erin. They care about the liability. If Moore is convicted, the family sues the Archdiocese. We're talking millions in damages. The PR fallout alone would be catastrophic."
"So I'm a janitor," Erin said. "Cleaning up a mess."
"You're a defense attorney," Karl corrected. "It's the same thing, just with better suits."

Erin looked at the photo of Emily again.
"Did he do it?" she asked.
"Did he kill her?" Karl shrugged. "Legally? Maybe. The negligence statute is broad. If he had a duty of care and he breached it... yes. But morally? He thinks he was saving her soul."
"Moral truth again," Erin said, a bitter taste in her mouth.
"It's going to be the central theme of the trial, Erin. Science vs. Faith. Medicine vs. Religion. Thomas is going to bring in doctors, psychiatrists, experts who will say Emily was epileptic and psychotic. He will say Moore was a dangerous fanatic."
"And what do I say?"
"You say that Emily Rose believed she was possessed. And that she had the right to choose her treatment. Even if that treatment was prayer."

"That's a weak defense, Karl. 'She wanted to die' doesn't play well with a jury."
"It's not 'she wanted to die'. It's 'she wanted to be saved'. There's a difference."
Karl leaned forward. His eyes were hard.
"This is the big one, Erin. The partners are watching. If you win this... the Senior Partnership is yours. Your name on the door. The corner office. The view."
He gestured to the window. The city lay spread out below them, grey and rain-swept.
"Everything you've worked for."

Erin looked at the view. It was beautiful. And cold.
"And if I lose?"
"Then you're the lawyer who let a priest go to jail for killing a teenage girl. You'll be toxic. You'll be doing traffic court in Skokie."
The threat was clear.
Erin closed the file.
"I'll take it."
"Good." Karl smiled. "He's at the County Jail. Go see him. But be careful, Erin."
"Careful of what? The press?"
"The priest," Karl said. "He's... intense. And from what I hear, he's not alone in that cell."
"What does that mean?"
Karl hesitated. For the first time, the shark looked unsure.
"It means," he said quietly, "that people who get close to this case... they tend to start seeing things. Hearing things."
"You're joking."
"Just watch your back, Erin. And don't let him get inside your head."

Erin stood up. She grabbed the file.
"I don't believe in ghosts, Karl. I believe in reasonable doubt."
"Let's hope the jury agrees," Karl said.

Erin walked out of the office.
The hallway seemed longer than usual.
The lights flickered overhead.
*Bzzzt.*
She paused.
She looked back at Karl's door.
It was closed.
She shook her head.
"Coffee," she muttered. "I need more coffee."
But as she walked to the elevator, the metallic taste in her mouth returned.
Stronger this time.
Like she was chewing on a penny.
