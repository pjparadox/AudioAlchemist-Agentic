# CHAPTER 1: NORTH DAKOTA (ULTRA-DENSITY)

**TIMELINE: EARTH-10005 (PRESENT DAY)**
**LOCATION: THE GRAVE OF JAMES HOWLETT**

The cold wasn't just a temperature. It was a texture.

It was a physical weight, pressing down on the North Dakota forest like a woolen blanket soaked in liquid nitrogen. It didn't just sit on the skin; it hunted. It sought out the weak points in my suit—the microscopic gaps in the stitching, the places where the spandex had thinned from friction—and it bit down with teeth made of wind shear.

I stood over the grave.

My breath didn't just fog; it crystallized. Tiny, jagged diamonds of moisture expelled from my lungs, freezing instantly in the air before drifting down to join the millions of other frozen breaths that made up this winter.

*I am a popsicle,* the voice in my head whispered. *I am a flavor of ice cream that nobody ordered. Cherry-red and regret-flavored.*

I adjusted my grip on the shovel. It was a cheap thing, bought from a hardware store three timelines over. The handle was ash wood, varnished to a shine that was already cracking under the thermal stress.

"Okay, Wolvie," I whispered. My voice sounded small, swallowed instantly by the vast, indifferent acoustics of the forest. "Don't be shy."

I looked at the ground.

It wasn't dirt. It was concrete made of soil. Permafrost. The earth here had frozen solid a thousand years ago and hadn't thawed since. It was a vault. A bank safe. And I was trying to break in with a gardening tool.

I raised the shovel.

*Physics check. Mass times acceleration equals force. Force divided by the resistance of frozen mud equals... pain.*

I brought it down.

*THWACK.*

The sound was obscene. It was the sound of a bone breaking under a heavy cloth.

The shockwave traveled up the wooden handle, bypassing my gloves, and slammed into the bones of my forearms. My radius and ulna vibrated like tuning forks.

"Motherfluffer," I hissed.

I looked at the ground. I had made a scratch. A tiny, insignificant white line in the dark earth.

*This is going to take a while.*

I swung again. And again. And again.

*Thwack. Thwack. Crunch.*

The rhythm took over.

Digging is a conversation between your muscles and the earth. And right now, the earth was winning.

My shoulders burned. The deltoids were screaming, filling with lactic acid that felt like battery fluid. My lats were tight cables of agony. But then... the heat.

The Healing Factor.

It kicked in. I could feel it. It was a biological hum, a swarm of microscopic bees working under my skin. It found the torn muscle fibers—the micro-traumas caused by the exertion—and it stitched them back together.

It wasn't pleasant. It was an itch I couldn't scratch. It was the sensation of pins and needles, dialed up to eleven.

*Dig. Tear. Heal. Dig. Tear. Heal.*

I was a machine of perpetual motion, fueled by self-repair and desperation.

The hole got deeper.

Six inches. A foot. Two feet.

The smell changed.

Topsoil smells like pine needles and squirrel shit. It's organic. Alive.
But deeper?
The layer below the frost line smelled different. It smelled of clay. Of wet slate. Of time. It was a heavy, mineral scent that coated the back of my throat and tasted like sucking on a penny.

"You're in there deep, aren't you, peanut?" I panted. Sweat was running down my back, freezing before it hit my waistband.

My shovel hit something.

*CLANG.*

It wasn't the dull thud of a rock. It was the ringing, high-pitched note of metal.

I stopped.

I dropped to my knees. The cold bit through the knees of my suit, instantly numbing my skin. I didn't care.

I clawed at the dirt with my gloved hands. I brushed away the frozen clumps of soil.

There it was.

The box.

It wasn't a coffin. Coffins are wood. They are polite. They decompose.
This was a crate. A shipping container for a soul.
It was made of an alloy that didn't rust, but it had tarnished. It was a dull, gunmetal grey, scarred by the weight of the earth pressing on it for years.

I ran my hand over the lid. It was cold enough to burn.

"Wakey wakey," I whispered. "Eggs and bakey."

I grabbed the edge of the lid.

I pulled.

It didn't budge. The hinges were fused. Time and pressure had welded them shut.

"Okay," I said. "Maximum Effort."

I planted my feet. I braced my back. I gripped the metal until my gloves creaked.

I pulled.

My biceps bulged. My triceps flared. I could feel the tendons in my neck popping, snapping like rubber bands, then instantly knitting back together. The pain was blinding. White hot stars danced in my vision.

*SCREEEEEEEEEEE.*

The sound was deafening. Metal shearing against metal. It sounded like a train braking on a track made of chalkboards. It was a sound that made your teeth ache.

The lid gave. Just an inch.

The smell hit me then.

I expected rot. I expected the sweet, cloying scent of decomposition.

But there was nothing.

Just... dust. Dry, sterile dust.

*He didn't rot,* I realized. *He desiccated.*

I threw the lid back. It slammed into the dirt with a heavy *thud*.

I looked inside.

The world narrowed down to the rectangle of the box. The forest, the snow, the cold—it all faded.

There was only the skeleton.

It was... beautiful.

I know, I know. That sounds morbid. Like I'm writing poetry for a goth girl in 2004. But it was.

It wasn't white. Bone is white. This was silver.
It was polished. Gleaming. Even in the dim light of the moon, it shone with an inner luminescence.
The Adamantium.
It coated every inch of him. Every rib was a silver arch. The femur was a chrome pillar. The skull...

I stared at the skull.

It was a masterpiece of engineering. The eye sockets were deep, shadowed pools. The jaw was set in a permanent, metallic grin.

*He died screaming,* the voice whispered. *Look at the jaw. It's unhinged. He died in pain.*

"No," I whispered. "No, no, no."

I reached out. I touched the skull.

It was smooth. frictionless. My finger slid over the brow ridge.

"You're dead," I said. The words felt heavy in my mouth, like stones. "You're actually dead."

I tapped the metal. *Tink. Tink.*

"I thought... I thought it was a metaphor," I said to the bones. "I thought you were just... sleeping. Healing. Taking a really long, dirt-nap."

I poked a rib.

"Regenerate!" I commanded. "Come on! Pop a claw! Heal! Do the thing!"

Nothing.

Just the silence of the grave. Just the wind whistling through the empty ribcage like a flute made of a superhero.

I sat back. I sat right in the mud.

The realization hit me not as a thought, but as a physical sensation. A hollowing out of my chest.

*He's gone. The Anchor is gone. My world is gone.*

"Freeze!"

The voice shattered the moment.

It came from everywhere. Amplified. Distorted by a megaphone.

I looked up.

They were standing on the rim of the clearing. Twelve of them.

TVA Minutemen.

Their armor was black, matte, absorbing the moonlight. Their helmets were faceless ovals of orange glass.
They held batons. Pruning sticks.
The tips glowed with a violent, violet energy. It hummed. *Vvvvvvm. Vvvvvvm.* It sounded like a nest of angry hornets.

"Wade Wilson," the lead agent said. His voice was tinny, filtered through a speaker. "Step away from the deceased Variant."

I looked at them. I looked at the box.

*Analysis:*
*   **Threat Level:** High. Those sticks erase you from existence.
*   **Escape Route:** None. Surrounded.
*   **Weaponry:** I have a shovel. They have god-tech.

I stood up. My knees popped.

"You guys," I said. "You have terrible timing. I'm having a moment here. A *Hamlet* moment. 'Alas, poor Logan! I knew him, Horatio!'"

I picked up the skull. I held it aloft.

"He was a fellow of infinite jest! Okay, mostly infinite grumpiness. But still."

"Drop the artifact," the agent ordered. "Hands on your head. Prepare for extraction."

"Extraction?" I laughed. It was a brittle, sharp sound. "Sounds like dentistry. And I hate dentists. They always ask you questions when your mouth is full of fingers."

I looked at the skeleton.

*I need a weapon.*

I looked at the femur.

The leg bone. The longest bone in the body. Coated in the strongest metal in the universe.

*It's a bat. A very expensive, very heavy baseball bat.*

"Sorry, peanut," I whispered to the skull. "I'm gonna need to borrow your legs. I promise I'll bring them back. Mostly."

I reached down. I grabbed the femur.

It was heavy. Denser than lead. It felt like holding a solid bar of gold.

I ripped it free. The connective tissue—dried, dusty tendons—snapped with a sound like tearing parchment.

"Prune him," the agent said.

The Minutemen advanced.

*Time slows down.*

Adrenaline is a hell of a drug. It floods the system. The world sharpened.
I saw the individual snowflakes drifting down.
I saw the condensation on the agent's visor.
I saw the exact arc of the first baton swinging toward my head.

"Music," I whispered. "I need a beat."

*BYE BYE BYE.*

The beat dropped in my head.

I moved.

*Beat 1: The Windup.*
I planted my left foot. I twisted my hips. I swung the femur like I was aiming for the fences at Yankee Stadium.
The air *whooshed*.

*Beat 2: The Impact.*
The bone connected with the agent's helmet.
*CRACK.*
It wasn't a clean sound. It was messy. Wet.
The helmet didn't just break; it shattered. The visor exploded into a thousand shards of orange glass.
The force of the blow was transferred instantly into the agent's neck. I saw the whiplash. I heard the vertebrae snapping like dry twigs.

*Beat 3: The Follow-Through.*
The agent flew. Literally flew. He hit a tree ten feet away. *THUD.* He slid down the bark, leaving a smear of something dark.

"Home run!" I yelled. "And the crowd goes wild!"

Two more rushed me.

I ducked. The first baton sizzled over my head. I could feel the heat of it. It smelled of ozone and singularitites. It singed the top of my mask.

I spun.

I drove the sharp end of the femur—the hip joint—into the second guy's gut.
The Adamantium didn't yield. The armor did.
It punched through the Kevlar. Through the padding. Through the stomach.
He gasped. A wet, gurgling sound.
I twisted the bone.
"Does it hurt?" I asked. "It looks like it hurts. You should put some ice on that. Oh wait, we're in North Dakota! It's all ice!"

I kicked him off the bone.

I was dancing now. The choreography of violence.

*Pirouette. Stab. Duck. Swing.*

My body was moving faster than my thoughts. I was a passenger in my own murder-machine.

I grabbed the skull again.

"Heads up!" I yelled.

I jammed the skull onto a guy's head. It fit perfectly over his helmet.
He clawed at it, blinded by the face of the Wolverine.
"Peek-a-boo!"
I punched the skull.
*BONG.*
It rang like a church bell. The agent crumpled, concussed by the echo chamber of Logan's head.

My breath came in ragged gasps. The cold air burned my throat. My muscles were on fire.

But I felt... *alive*.

For the first time in six years, I wasn't Wade the Salesman. I wasn't the guy in the beige suit.

I was the Merc with a Mouth. I was the Regenerating Degenerate.

I was Deadpool.

And I was having a ball.
# CHAPTER 2: THE MASK THAT DOESN'T FIT (ULTRA-DENSITY)

**TIMELINE: EARTH-10005 (SIX YEARS LATER)**
**LOCATION: WILSON MOTORS**

The wig didn't just itch. It conspired.

It sat on my head like a dead animal that was slowly waking up. The mesh lining was a grid of sandpaper, digging into the keloid scars of my scalp with a persistence that felt personal. It was a torture device sold as a hairpiece, a Toupee of Damocles that threatened to slide off at the first sign of sudden movement or moderate humidity.

I stood in the center of the showroom.

Wilson Motors.

The name itself was a joke. A cosmic pun. Wade Wilson. Wilson Motors. It sounded like a front for a money-laundering operation run by a guy who watched too much *Breaking Bad*. But it wasn't. It was real. It was aggressively, banally real.

The air in the room was stagnant. It was a cocktail of scents that no amount of air conditioning could scrub away:
*   **Top Note:** Industrial floor wax. Lemon-scented chemical warfare.
*   **Middle Note:** Stale coffee. Burnt. Acrid. The kind of coffee that hates you for drinking it.
*   **Base Note:** The musk of desperation. The specific, pheromonal stink of men who work on commission and haven't hit their quota in three months.

"So," the customer said.

He was a man named Gary (not my boss, a different Gary, let's call him Customer-Gary). He was forty-two. He wore a polo shirt tucked into khaki shorts that were aggressively beige. He had the calves of a man who owned a Peloton but never turned it on.

"Does it have the... you know?" Customer-Gary asked.

He made a hand gesture. A vague, circular wave that could have meant anything from "heated seats" to "a button that launches nuclear missiles."

I knew what he meant. They always meant the same thing.

"The sliding doors?" I asked.

My voice sounded wrong.

It was smooth. Polished. It lacked the grit, the gravel, the beautiful chaotic rasp that usually accompanied a katana swing. It was the voice of a man who owned a steam mop. It was the voice of Wade Wilson, Regional Sales Associate (Bronze Tier).

"Yeah," Customer-Gary said. "The kids, you know? They kick the doors."

"Sir," I said. I patted the hood of the white Kia Carnival.

*Thump.*

The sound was hollow. Tinny. It echoed in the cavernous showroom like a drumbeat of mediocrity.

"Let me tell you about the Carnival," I said. "This isn't just a minivan. This is a fortress of solitude on wheels. It has automatic smart-power sliding doors. You approach the vehicle with the key fob in your pocket? Boom. Sesame opens. No hands required."

I leaned in. I lowered my voice to a conspiratorial whisper.

"Because let's be honest, Gary. Your hands are full. Groceries. Screaming toddlers. The crushing weight of the choices you made in your twenties when you thought you were going to be a rock star but ended up in middle management."

Gary laughed.

It was a dry sound. Like stepping on a dead leaf.

"Funny guy," Gary said. "You're a funny guy, Wade."

"I try," I said.

My face hurt.

It was the smile. I had been smiling for six hours. My zygomaticus major muscles were trembling from the exertion. Under the mask of my face—not the red mask, the human mask—my skin felt tight.

*Kill him,* the voice in my head whispered. *Just a little bit. Use the stapler on his desk. It’s right there. Swingline. Red. Heavy.*

I visualized it.

*The Fantasy:*
I grab the stapler. I vault over the hood of the Kia. I drive the stapler into Gary's forehead. *KA-CHUNK.*
"That's for the khaki shorts!" I scream.
I rip off the wig. I throw it into the ceiling fan.
I steal the Kia. I drive it through the plate glass window. *CRASH.*
I am free. I am chaos.

*The Reality:*
"It also has Apple CarPlay," I said. "Wireless."

"Nice," Gary said. "Does it come in blue?"

"It comes in 'Ceramic Silver'," I said. "Which is grey. Everything is grey, Gary. It's the color of the soul."

"I'll take the grey," Gary said.

I processed the paperwork.

It took forty minutes. Forty minutes of typing. Forty minutes of listening to the dot-matrix printer scream as it birthed another contract.

*Reee-errrr-reeee-errrr.*

When Gary left, I walked to the window.

It was raining outside.

Not a dramatic storm. Not the kind of rain you fight a supervillain in. This was a drizzle. A grey, miserable mist that coated the world in a layer of grime. The parking lot was a sea of wet asphalt and unsold inventory. Rows and rows of cars, staring at me with dead headlights.

I looked at my reflection in the glass.

Wade Wilson. Toupee (slightly askew). Beige suit (polyester blend, flammable). Name tag (crooked).

I looked... fine. Normal.

But underneath?

I rubbed my wrist. The bone ached. It was a dull throb, a reminder of the Adamantium handcuffs I wasn't wearing, the katanas I wasn't holding.

My skin was a map of pain. Under the cheap suit, my body was a topographic representation of a cancer ward. Lumpy. Scarred. It looked like an avocado had sex with an older, uglier avocado.

And under the skin?

*Empty.*

There was a hole in me. A void. And I was filling it with Honda Civics and lies.

"Wilson!"

The shout came from the manager's office.

Gary. My boss. (Yes, everyone is named Gary today. It's a glitch in the simulation).

I turned.

"Gary! My man! Looking... adequate today. Very symmetrical."

Gary sat behind his desk. He looked tired. He had bags under his eyes that could carry the weekly shopping.

"The upsells, Wilson," Gary sighed. He rubbed his temples. "You sold the Carnival, but you didn't push the rust protection. Rust is the enemy."

"Rust is nature, Gary," I said. "Entropy. Dust to dust. Rust to rust. Who are we to fight God?"

"We are a dealership, Wilson," Gary said. "We fight God for margin. Just sell the coating."

"Yes, sir."

I walked back to my desk.

I sat down. The chair squeaked. *Eeee-errrk.*

I opened my drawer.

Inside, hidden under a stack of brochures for the 2024 Sorento and a bag of stale pretzels, was a polaroid.

Vanessa.

She was smiling. It wasn't a "customer service" smile. It was real. It reached her eyes.

I touched the photo. My finger shook.

*I promised her,* I thought. *No more chaos. No more masks. Just Wade. Just this.*

I closed the drawer.

I looked around the office.

At the next desk, Peter was on the phone.

Peter. My best friend. My only friend who didn't try to kill me or sleep with me.

"No, ma'am, the floormats are extra," Peter said. He sounded so sincere. "But they are high-quality rubber. They capture... spills."

Peter was happy. He had a locker full of photos of his cats. He had a sugar glider named Muffin. He had a life. He had content.

I had a wig and a lie.

*I am the B-story of my own life,* I realized. *I am the comic relief in a tragedy that nobody is watching.*

I stood up.

"I'm taking a break," I announced.

"You just got here," Gary called out.

"Diarrhea," I said loudly. "Explosive. Unpredictable. Like the third act of a superhero movie. You don't want to be in the splash zone, Gary."

I walked to the bathroom.

I locked the door.

The bathroom smelled of bleach and something that had died in the ventilation system.

I leaned against the sink. I stared at myself in the mirror.

"You're doing great," I told the reflection. "You're nailing it. You're a productive member of society. You have a 401k. You have a dental plan."

The reflection stared back. The eyes were dead. Blue chips of ice in a melted face.

"Liar," the reflection whispered.

I gripped the sink. The porcelain was cold.

I wanted to scream. I wanted to peel the face off. I wanted to reach into the mirror and strangle the man in the beige suit.

But I didn't.

I straightened my tie. I adjusted my wig.

I flushed the toilet (for sound effects).

I unlocked the door and walked back out into the grey.
# CHAPTER 3: THE QUIET KNIFE (ULTRA-DENSITY)

**POV: VANESSA CARLYSLE**
**TIMELINE: EARTH-10005 (PRESENT DAY)**
**LOCATION: THE APARTMENT**

The apartment didn't smell like home anymore. It smelled like a performance.

It smelled of cheap tequila (the kind that burns the nose before it hits the tongue), taco seasoning that was mostly salt and red dye #40, and the humid, over-sweet scent of fifty bodies crammed into a space designed for two.

I stood in the doorway of the kitchen, holding a bowl of guacamole that Blind Al had already insulted three times.

"It's too chunky!" she had screamed, waving a chip like a dagger. "I want paste! I want green sludge!"

I looked past Al. I looked past Peter, who was showing Colossus a picture of his cat with a level of earnestness that was almost painful.

I looked at Wade.

He was standing near the hallway mirror. He thought he was alone.

He wasn't looking at the party. He was looking at his reflection. He was adjusting that terrible, lumpy toupee—a piece of synthetic hair that sat on his scarred scalp like a nervous badger.

I watched him practice.

He stretched his lips wide. *Smile.*
He held it for a count of three.
Then he let it drop. His face went slack. The light went out of his eyes.
Then he did it again.
*Smile. Hold. Drop.*

It was mechanical. It was the same way he used to practice reloading his Desert Eagles. Muscle memory. Repetition until the action became instinct.

But this wasn't a reload. This was a lie.

A sharp pain twisted in my chest. Not a heartburn pain. A grief pain. A quiet knife sliding between my ribs.

*He’s dying,* I thought. *Not the cancer. The cancer is gone. But the spark? The chaos? The thing that made him Wade? It’s starving.*

I remembered the first time I met him. The bar. The swagger. The way he took up space not because he was big, but because he was *loud*. He was a disaster, sure. A walking collection of red flags sewn into a quilt. But he was a *beautiful* disaster. He was alive. He burned bright enough to light up the dark corners of my own life.

Now?

Now he was a car salesman named Wade who wore beige.

He was doing it for me. I knew that. He was trying to be the man he thought I wanted. The safe man. The man who came home at 5:30 PM with nothing on his hands but ink stains. The man who could give me a family without the risk of mercenaries kicking down the door.

But I missed him.

God help me, I missed the mercenary. I missed the edge. I missed the dangerous, unpredictable idiot who made me laugh until I couldn't breathe.

*Be careful what you wish for,* my mother used to say. *You might get a husband who sells Kias.*

Wade sighed. I saw his shoulders slump. It was a heavy motion, like he was carrying the weight of the entire world's mediocrity on his back.

"Happy Birthday to me," he whispered to the mirror.

His voice broke. Just a little. A tiny crack in the porcelain.

I couldn't watch anymore. It felt like an intrusion. Like watching someone pray to a god they didn't believe in.

I took a deep breath. I pasted a smile on my face. A real one. Because I did love him. I loved him enough to play along. I loved him enough to pretend I didn't see the cracks.

"Hey, handsome," I called out.

Wade spun around.

The transformation was instant. Terrifyingly fast.

The mask slammed down. The "Salesman Wade" persona clicked into place like a visor. His eyes widened. The smile became fixed. The posture straightened.

"Ness!" He beamed. "You caught me! Just... admiring the merchandise. High resale value on this guy. Low mileage. Only driven on Sundays by a little old lady from Pasadena."

He walked over to me.

He kissed me.

His lips were warm. They tasted of the cheap beer he'd been nursing all night.

But his eyes?

His eyes were screaming. They were panic-stricken, blue chips of ice searching mine for approval, for validation. *Do you believe me? Am I doing it right? Is this what normal looks like?*

"Everyone is here," I said, keeping my voice light. "Blind Al is arguing with the guacamole."

"Classic Al," Wade said. "She hates texture. If it's not a slurry, it's a threat."

He took my hand.

His grip was loose. Careful. Like he was afraid he might crush me if he held on too tight. Like he was afraid his own strength—his own nature—would ruin the illusion.

"Let's go party," he said.

"Wade," I said.

He stopped. "Yeah?"

"You okay?"

I asked it, but I already knew the answer. I wanted him to tell me the truth. I wanted him to drop the mask. I wanted him to say, *No, Ness. I'm miserable. I hate the wig. I hate the cars. I want to stab something.*

If he said that, maybe we could fix it. Maybe we could find a middle ground.

But he didn't.

"Me?" He laughed. It was a brittle sound. "Never better. I'm living the dream, baby. The American Dream. Credit debt, lower back pain, and a lingering fear of the IRS."

He winked.

It was a lie. A perfect, polished, heartbreaking lie.

*I have to let him go,* I thought. The realization hit me with the force of a physical blow. *If I love him, I have to let him go. I can't keep him in this cage. I can't watch him fade away until there's nothing left but the beige.*

I squeezed his hand.

"Okay," I said. "Let's go."

We walked into the living room.

The party was loud. The music—some pop hit from five years ago—was thumping.

Dopinder was trying to flirt with a girl who looked terrified. Negasonic was texting with a ferocity that suggested she was coordinating a drone strike. Yukio waved, radiating pink energy and genuine joy.

It was a family. A weird, broken, beautiful family.

But Wade... Wade looked like a ghost haunting his own life.

He moved through the room like a guest. He laughed at the right times. He made the right jokes. But he wasn't *present*.

He grabbed a chimichanga. He took a bite.

"Mmm," he said. "Moist."

He looked at the window.

Outside, the rain was falling. Grey, miserable rain.

His eyes lingered on the dark glass. He was looking for something out there. A threat. A villain. A plot twist. Anything to break the monotony.

*Knock. Knock. Knock.*

The sound cut through the laughter.

Three sharp raps. Precise. Authoritative. Not the knock of a neighbor complaining about the noise. Not the knock of a pizza delivery guy.

This was a knock that meant business.

Wade froze.

I saw it. The shift. The tension in his shoulders coiled tight. The way his weight shifted to the balls of his feet. The way his hand drifted toward his hip, reaching for a holster that wasn't there.

For a second, the Salesman was gone. The Mercenary was back.

And he looked... relieved.

"I'll get it," Wade said.

His voice was different. Deeper. calmer.

He walked to the door.

I watched him go. And I knew.

*This is it. This is the end of the quiet.*

I felt a tear slide down my cheek. I didn't wipe it away.

*Go,* I whispered to his back. *Go be who you are.*
# CHAPTER 4: THE AVENGER PITCH (ULTRA-DENSITY)

**TIMELINE: EARTH-616 (THE SACRED TIMELINE)**
**DATE: MARCH 14, 2018**
**LOCATION: STARK TOWER**

The elevator ride was a lesson in physics and anxiety.

I could feel the G-force pressing down on me, a gentle hand pushing me toward the floor. But the anxiety? The anxiety was a boot on my neck.

It was quiet. Not the good kind of quiet. Not the "we just saved the world and are enjoying the silence before the shawarma" quiet. It was the "I am about to ask the coolest kid in school to prom and I am wearing a diaper made of bees" kind of quiet.

I checked my watch.

It was a Hello Kitty watch. Pink strap. Rhinestones. It had been a gift from a blind woman who thought it was a Rolex.

*Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.*

The sound was deafening in the brushed-steel box. It sounded like a bomb timer.

*Okay, Wade. Focus. You are Wade Wilson. You are a special forces operative. You have killed dictators with a Zamboni. You have regenerated from a pile of ash. You can do this.*

I adjusted my tie.

I wasn't wearing the suit. I was wearing *the* suit.

No, not the red one. The red one smells like blood and chimichangas. This was a business suit. Charcoal grey. Sharp lapels. A silk tie that cost more than my first car. I looked like I was about to defend a mob boss in court, or perhaps sell a very aggressive timeshare.

The doors opened.

*Ding.*

Stark Tower. Or Avengers Tower. Or whatever they call it this week. The branding changes every time the toys stop selling.

The lobby hit me with a wall of sensory information.

**Smell:** Money. It smelled of ozone, polished marble, and vibranium. It smelled like Elon Musk's cologne, if Elon Musk actually showered and didn't smell like musk (ironic, really).
**Sight:** Glass. Chrome. Light. Everything was reflective. I saw a hundred distorted Wades staring back at me from the walls, the floor, the ceiling.
**Sound:** A low, hum. The building itself was alive. Powered by an Arc Reactor. It buzzed with the energy of a thousand suns contained in a donut.

I walked to the reception desk.

The receptionist was a hologram? No. Just very shiny. She looked at me with eyes that had seen gods and aliens and was unimpressed by a guy in a charcoal suit.

"Wade Wilson," I said. My voice cracked. I cleared my throat. "Wade Wilson. Here to see Happy Hogan."

She didn't blink. "Do you have an appointment?"

"I have a destiny," I said. "And a referral. From Dopinder. He drives a taxi. He knows people. He drove Tony Stark once. Or maybe it was Tony Danza. Dopinder gets confused."

She sighed. It was a long, weary sigh. "Floor 42."

I got back in the elevator.

*Floor 42. The answer to life, the universe, and everything. That's a good sign. Unless it's a Douglas Adams reference, in which case the planet is about to be demolished to make way for a hyperspace bypass.*

The doors closed. The numbers climbed.

*38... 39... 40...*
