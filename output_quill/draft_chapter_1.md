# CHAPTER 1: THE COMMUTE

The air in the showroom smelled of stale coffee, floor wax, and the desperate optimism of sub-prime lending. It was a specific cocktail of scents, one that coated the back of Wade Wilson’s throat like a film of cheap grease.

He adjusted his tie. It was a polyester monstrosity, wide enough to land a plane on, patterned with little sailboats that seemed to be sinking. It was the kind of tie a man wore when he had given up on being a superhero and decided that his true calling was moving mid-sized family vehicles with best-in-class safety ratings.

"So." The customer shifted his weight, testing the floor tiles with his heels. He was a man in his forties, soft around the middle, wearing a polo shirt that was tucked in aggressively tight. "Does it have the... you know?"

He made a vague hand gesture that could have meant anything from heated seats to a portal to the dark dimension.

Wade smiled. It was the smile he had practiced in the mirror for six weeks. The 'I am a trustworthy human being with a pulse and a credit score' smile. It hurt his face. Underneath the wig—a masterful piece of toupee engineering that sat on his scarred scalp like a nervous badger—his skin felt tight.

"The sliding doors?" Wade asked, voice smooth, devoid of the manic rasp that usually accompanied a katana swing. "Sir, let me tell you about the Carnival. It doesn't just have sliding doors. It has *automatic smart-power* sliding doors. You approach the vehicle with the key fob in your pocket? Boom. Sesame opens. No hands required. Because let's be honest, your hands are full. Groceries. Screaming children. The crushing weight of existence."

The customer blinked. "Right. The kids."

"Exactly." Wade patted the hood of the white minivan. It rang hollow, a tinny sound that echoed in the cavernous dealership. "Look at this bad boy. V6 engine. ample cargo space. You could fit... well, you could fit a lot of soccer equipment in here. Or lumber. Or, hypothetically, if you were a different kind of person, which you aren't, several duffel bags of non-sequential bills."

The man laughed nervously. "Funny guy."

Wade leaned back against the minivan. "I try." The humor felt dry in his mouth. Ashy.

*This is it. This is the dream.*

He looked out the plate glass window. The world outside was grey. Rain drizzled on the asphalt, turning the parking lot into a watercolor painting of misery. No explosions. No ninjas. No time-traveling cyborgs trying to kill a kid. Just cars. Just the endless, grinding cycle of commerce.

His wrist itched.

Not the skin. The bone. The place where the scars ran deepest, where the cancer had chewed on him like a dog with a bone. He rubbed it absentmindedly, feeling the lumpy texture through the cheap fabric of his suit jacket.

"Wade!"

The shout came from the manager's office. Gary. Or was it Larry? It was definitely a name that ended in a vowel sound that implied disappointment.

"Excuse me one moment." Wade pushed off the van. "Browse the infotainment system. It has Apple CarPlay. It will change your life. Or at least distract you from it."

He walked across the showroom floor. His loafers squeaked. *Squeak. Squeak. Squeak.*

The office was a glass box in the corner. A fishbowl for the big fish to watch the little fish swim in circles. Gary-Larry was sitting behind a desk that was too big for the room, staring at a spreadsheet.

"Wilson." He didn't look up. "Your numbers."

"Stellar? Astronomical? Defying the laws of physics?"

"Adequate." Gary-Larry looked up. He had eyes like a dead cod. "You sold the Honda Odyssey last week. That was good. But you're lagging on the upsells. The extended warranties. The undercoating."

"The undercoating is a scam, Gary," Wade said. "It's literally just spray paint and lies."

"It's *peace of mind*," Gary corrected. "And it's margin. We need margin, Wilson. You want to keep this job? You want to keep playing... whatever character this is?" He gestured vaguely at Wade's face. "Then sell the rust protection."

Wade stared at him.

For a second—just a microsecond—the world tilted.

He saw it. He saw the red targeting reticle over Gary's forehead. He saw the trajectory of a staple gun from the desk. He saw the cinematic slow-motion sequence where he vaulted the desk, kicked the ergonomic chair through the window, and rode the coffee machine into the sunset.

*Do it,* the voice in his head whispered. *Just a little maiming. As a treat.*

Wade swallowed. He forced the smile back onto his face. He locked the reticle away. He put the staple gun back in the drawer of his mind.

"Rust protection," Wade said. "Got it. Rust never sleeps. Neither do I. We're birds of a feather."

"Get back out there," Gary said, dismissing him with a wave.

Wade walked out.

He went to the break room. He poured a cup of coffee that had been sitting on the burner since the Carter administration. It tasted like burnt rubber and regret.

He leaned against the counter and closed his eyes.

He touched the locket under his shirt. The picture of Vanessa.

*I promised,* he thought. *No more chaos. No more violence. Just Wade. Just... this.*

He took a sip of the coffee.

It scalded his tongue.

"Ow," he whispered.

He opened his eyes. He looked at his reflection in the microwave door. The wig was slightly askew.

"You look great," he told the blurry reflection. "You look like a million bucks. Adjusted for inflation."

He straightened his tie. He walked back out to the floor.

"Sir!" he called out to the customer, his voice booming with forced cheer. "Let's talk about floor mats! You're gonna want the all-weather ones. Trust me. Life gets messy."
