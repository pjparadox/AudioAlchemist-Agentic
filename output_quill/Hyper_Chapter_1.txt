# THE EXORCISM OF EMILY ROSE

## BOOK I: THE COVENANT

### CHAPTER 1: THE PREDATOR

The courtroom smelled of floor wax, old wood, and the specific, acrid sweat of a man who knows he is about to lose everything.

Erin Bruner stood at the defense table, her hand resting lightly on the mahogany. She didn't look at the jury. Not yet. She looked at the prosecutor, a man named Miller who was currently sweating through his cheap polyester suit. Miller was droning on about "justice" and "societal debt," words that meant nothing in this room. In this room, there was only one currency: Doubt.

And Erin was rich.

She glanced at her client, James Van Hopper. He was a small man with eyes like dead fish—flat, black, and devoid of anything resembling a soul. He was guilty. Erin knew it. Karl Gunderson, the senior partner watching from the gallery, knew it. Even the bailiff, who was currently fighting a losing battle against sleep, knew it. Van Hopper had killed his wife. He had strangled her with a telephone cord in a fit of rage because she burned the roast.

But "knowing" and "proving" were two different countries, and Erin held the passport between them.

She remembered the cross-examination of Mrs. Higgins two days ago. That had been the turning point. The old woman had sat in the witness box, clutching her purse like a shield.

*"Mrs. Higgins,"* Erin had said, her voice soft, almost matronly. *"You stated that you saw Mr. Van Hopper's car leave the driveway at 5:45 PM. Is that correct?"*
*"Yes, ma'am. I was watching 'Days of Our Lives'. It goes to commercial at 5:45."*
*"I see. And you watched from your living room window?"*
*"Yes."*
Erin had walked to the defense table and picked up a photograph.
*"This is a photo of your living room window, taken from the street. There is a large oak tree in the front yard. Is that correct?"*
*"Yes."*
*"And on October 14th... the leaves would still be on the tree, wouldn't they?"*
Mrs. Higgins had hesitated. *"I... suppose so."*
*"And it was raining that day. Heavy rain."*
*"It was drizzling."*
*"Actually,"* Erin produced a weather report, *"it was raining 1.2 inches an hour. A downpour. So, Mrs. Higgins... you claim you saw a car... through a rain-slicked window... through the branches of an oak tree... from fifty feet away."*
*"I know what I saw."*
*"Do you? Or do you know what the police *told* you that you saw?"*
Mrs. Higgins had flushed red. *"He's a bad man! I heard them fighting!"*
*"Objection,"* Erin had said, bored. *"Hearsay."*

That was the moment. The moment the jury decided that maybe, just maybe, the old lady was confused. And if she was confused about the car, maybe she was confused about the screaming.

"Ms. Bruner?" Judge Halloway asked. "Your closing?"

Erin moved. She didn't walk; she glided. It was a practiced movement, designed to draw every eye in the room. She wore a tailored grey suit—Armani, bought with the bonus from the Carruthers acquittal—that was professional enough to command respect but feminine enough to disarm the older men on the jury.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Erin began. Her voice was low, forcing the jurors to lean in. It was a trick she learned in her first year. *Make them come to you.* "Mr. Miller talks about justice. He talks about the tragedy of Mrs. Van Hopper's death. And it is a tragedy."

She paused, letting the silence stretch until it was almost uncomfortable. The air conditioning hummed, a low thrum that sounded like a mechanical heartbeat.

"But we are not here to mourn. We are here to determine facts. And the fact is, the timeline doesn't fit."

She walked to the timeline exhibit. A simple piece of foam board that was about to save a murderer.

"The State claims Mr. Van Hopper was home at 6:00 PM. But Mrs. Higgins, the neighbor... the State's own witness... testified she saw his car leaving at 5:45. Fifteen minutes." Erin turned to the jury, locking eyes with Juror Number 4, a schoolteacher who had been nodding along with Miller earlier. "Fifteen minutes. That is the difference between a murderer and a grieving husband. Can you be sure? Can you look at this man and say, with absolute certainty, that Mrs. Higgins—a woman who admitted she wasn't wearing her glasses—saw what she claims she saw?"

She saw the doubt bloom in Juror 4's eyes. It was a physical thing, a subtle shift in posture, a slight furrowing of the brow.

*Got you,* Erin thought.

"If you have even a shadow of a doubt," Erin whispered, "you must acquit. That is the law. That is the promise we make to every citizen. Even the ones we don't like."

She sat down.
Van Hopper leaned over. "That was good," he whispered. His breath smelled of mints and rot. A smell that stuck in the back of your throat.
"Don't talk to me," Erin said, her voice devoid of warmth. "Look at the table. Look sad."
"I *am* sad, Erin. I loved her."
Erin didn't look at him. She looked at her fingernails. Perfect. Manicured.
*You loved her so much you strangled her until her eyes popped,* she thought. *But the State didn't find the cord. And without the cord, they have nothing.*

The jury filed out.
The waiting began.
This was the part Erin hated. The limbo.
She sat in the hallway, drinking a lukewarm coffee from the vending machine. It tasted like burnt plastic and sugar.
Karl sat next to her.
"You got them," he said.
"Number 4 is wavering. He's a moralist."
"He's a teacher. He likes logic. You gave him logic."
"I gave him a loophole, Karl. There's a difference."

Karl laughed. He lit a cigarette, ignoring the 'No Smoking' sign. The smoke curled up, blue and grey in the fluorescent light.
"Legal truth is the only truth that matters, Erin. Did the State prove its case? No. Therefore, James Van Hopper is innocent."
"Is he?"
"In the eyes of the law? Yes. In the eyes of God? That's not our department."

Erin watched the smoke.
*God,* she thought. *I haven't thought about Him since I was twelve.*
She grew up Catholic. She remembered the incense. The statues. The feeling of being watched.
But law school had burned that out of her. Law school taught her that there was no divine judge. There was just a judge appointed by the Governor, who probably cheated on his taxes.

"The jury is back," the bailiff called out.

The courtroom was silent.
"We the jury," the foreman read, his voice shaking slightly.
Erin held her breath. This was the drug. The rush. The moment where life and death hung on a single word.
"...find the defendant, James Van Hopper..."
Erin looked at Van Hopper. He was squeezing his eyes shut.
"...Not Guilty."

A gasp from the gallery. Mrs. Van Hopper's sister burst into tears.
"No! He did it! You know he did it!"
The judge banged his gavel. "Order! Order in this court!"

Erin didn't smile. She didn't pump her fist. She simply nodded, a mask of professional solemnity. But inside, the adrenaline hit her like a drug. It was better than sex. It was better than love. It was the pure, uncut high of winning.

She packed her briefcase, ignoring Van Hopper's outstretched hand.
"You're a wizard, Erin," he said.
"I'm a lawyer, James," she said, snapping the locks shut. "There's a difference. Pay the bill."

She walked out of the courtroom, the heels of her shoes clicking a rhythm on the marble floor. *Click-clack. Click-clack.* The sound of a predator leaving the kill site.
Mrs. Van Hopper's sister was waiting in the hallway.
She lunged at Erin.
"How do you sleep?" she screamed, spit flying from her mouth. "How do you sleep at night knowing what you did?"
Security guards grabbed her, pulling her back.
Erin stopped. She looked at the woman.
"I sleep just fine," Erin said.
It was a lie.
But it was a good one.
She pushed through the revolving doors, out into the city.
It was raining. A cold, grey drizzle that slicked the streets.
Erin opened her umbrella. A black dome against the grey sky.
She hailed a cab.
"Where to, lady?" the driver asked.
"The Gavel," she said. "And step on it. I need a drink."
She looked out the window as the city blurred by.
She had won.
So why did she feel like she had just lost something important?
Something she couldn't even name.
