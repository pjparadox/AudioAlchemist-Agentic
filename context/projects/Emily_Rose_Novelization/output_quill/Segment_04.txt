Chapter 4: The Sound of One Hand Clapping

Dr. Edith Vogel was small, with hands that fluttered like sparrows when she spoke. She sat in the witness box, looking like she belonged in a library, surrounded by dusty books and silence, not here, under the harsh fluorescent glare of the courtroom.

Owen Thomas paced in front of her. He was building a cage, bar by bar.

"I've known Emily her entire life," Vogel said. Her voice was thin, reedy. "She was a bit sickly as a child. But happy. Always reading."

"Did you see her before she left for the University?" Owen asked.

"Yes. For her physical."

"And her health?"

"Perfect." Vogel hesitated. "A bit thin. But perfect."

Erin watched the jury. They liked Vogel. She was the grandmother everyone wanted—kind, observant, harmless. That made her dangerous.

"Did she confide in you?" Owen asked.

"Yes. She was nervous about leaving. It was a big change. Leaving the farm. Leaving her family."

"And did she contact you after she left?"

"Her mother called me," Vogel said. "At 4:00 AM. She woke me up. She said Emily was hysterical. She put her on the phone."

"Where was Emily?"

"At a payphone on campus. In her nightgown. In the freezing cold."

*Flashback.*

The campus was a labyrinth of stone and shadow. The wind cut through Emily's thin nightshirt like a knife. She stood at the payphone, the receiver pressed to her ear, shivering so hard her teeth clicked together.

"Dr. Vogel?" she sobbed. "I... I don't know what's happening."

"Emily? What is it? Calm down, child."

"I woke up," Emily whispered. "The smell... it was burning. Like hair. Like meat."

She looked around. The quad was empty. The streetlights cast long, distorted shadows that seemed to stretch toward her.

"I thought there was a fire," she said. "But there wasn't. There was no one."

*Present Day.*

"She told me about the dorm room," Vogel said. "She said she went back to bed. And then..." Vogel paused, her hands twisting in her lap. "She said something was in the room with her."

"Something?" Owen asked.

"A presence. She said she felt a weight. On her chest."

"Like a person?"

"Like... something heavy. She couldn't breathe. She couldn't move."

Owen nodded. He turned to the jury. "Sleep paralysis. A common symptom of stress. Of exhaustion."

He dismissed Vogel and called Dr. Anton Marek.

Marek was the opposite of Vogel. He was slick, modern, a man who believed the brain was just a computer made of meat.

"I suspected drugs," Marek said, shrugging. "LSD. Mushrooms. But the tox screen was clean."

"So what was your diagnosis?"

"Epilepsy," Marek said. "Psychomotor epilepsy. It causes seizures. Hallucinations. Muscle rigidity."

"And the weight she felt?"

"A seizure," Marek said. "The muscles contract. The chest tightens. The brain interprets this as an external force. A demon sitting on your chest. It's a classic hallucination."

Erin scribbled on her legal pad. *Classic hallucination.* It sounded so tidy. So safe.

"And the treatment?" Owen asked.

"Tegretol. An anti-convulsant."

"Did it work?"

"It would have," Marek said, his voice sharpening. "If she had taken it."

"Why didn't she?"

Marek looked at Father Moore. His gaze was accusatory. "She told me her priest said it was a spiritual condition. Not a medical one."

"So she stopped the medication?"

"Yes."

"And the result?"

"She died," Marek said. "The seizures continued. They got worse. They destroyed her body. She starved. She broke her own bones."

Owen let the silence hang in the air. "Thank you, Doctor."

He walked back to his table, looking grim.

Erin stood up. She smoothed her skirt. She walked to the witness box.

"Dr. Marek," she said. "You said the weight on her chest was a hallucination?"

"Yes."

"And the objects moving?"

Marek frowned. "What objects?"

"She told you a cup of pens moved across her desk. On its own. Is that a symptom of epilepsy?"

"No."

"Is it a symptom of psychosis?"

"It... could be."

"So she was epileptic *and* psychotic?" Erin asked. "Two separate severe mental illnesses striking a perfectly healthy nineteen-year-old girl at the exact same moment?"

Marek stiffened. "It's rare. But possible."

"More possible than... something else?"

"Objection," Owen said, not looking up.

"Withdrawn," Erin said.

She looked at the jury. They looked confused. Confusion was good. Confusion was doubt.

Chapter 5: The Face in the Rain

The trial dragged on. Rain lashed the courthouse windows, a relentless grey curtain.

Jason, Emily's boyfriend, took the stand. He looked like a boy who had aged ten years in ten months. He wore a suit that was too big for him, his hands lost in the sleeves.

"She was normal," Jason said quietly. "At first. She was smart. Funny. We... we liked to dance."

Erin nodded. "Tell us about the hospital."

"After the dorm thing," Jason said. "She went for tests. I went to visit her."

*Flashback.*

The hospital room smelled of antiseptic and lemon Jell-O. Emily sat on the bed, looking small.

She was singing. A hymn. Her voice was thin, fragile.

"Tender shepherd, hear me..."

She looked out the window. The sun was setting, painting the sky in bruises of purple and red.

Clouds gathered on the horizon. Dark, roiling storm clouds.

Emily stopped singing. She stood up. She walked to the window.

The clouds were moving. Not with the wind. Against it.

They swirled, knitting themselves together. A nose. A mouth. Eyes.

A face formed in the sky. Massive. Ancient.

It leered at her. It opened its mouth, a cavern of darkness that swallowed the light.

Emily stumbled back. She knocked over her food tray. *Clatter.*

Jason walked in. He held a bouquet of cheap daisies. "Emily?"

She turned.

For a second—just a split second—her face wasn't her face.

It was a skull. The skin pulled tight, translucent. The eyes were black holes. The teeth were jagged, broken shards.

Jason dropped the flowers.

"Emily?"

She blinked. The face was gone. She was just a girl again, terrified, shaking.

"They're here," she whispered. "They found me."

*Present Day.*

"She saw faces everywhere," Jason told the jury. "In the rain. In the condensation on windows. In people."

"People?" Erin asked.

"Strangers," Jason said. "Students walking to class. She said their faces would... change. Melt. Become something else."

"Like what?"

"Like demons," Jason whispered. "She said the world was full of them. Hiding in plain sight."

The courtroom was silent. The rain drummed against the glass. *Tap. Tap. Tap.*

Like fingers.

Chapter 6: The Tape

The prosecution rested. They had painted a picture of a sick girl and a negligent priest. It was a strong case. A logical case.

Erin needed a miracle. Or a nightmare.

"The defense calls Father Richard Moore."

Moore walked to the stand. He moved with a quiet dignity, ignoring the glares from the gallery.

"Father," Erin said. "You performed an exorcism on October 31st."

"Yes."

"And you recorded it?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"To document the truth," Moore said. "For the Church. And for Emily."

Erin picked up a plastic bag. Inside was a micro-cassette tape.

"Your Honor," she said. "I wish to enter this into evidence. Defense Exhibit A."

Owen Thomas stood up. "Objection! We haven't heard this tape."

"Neither have I," Erin lied. "It was provided by a witness who... wished to remain anonymous until now."

"Dr. Cartwright," Moore said.

The name hung in the air.

"He was there," Moore said. "A medical doctor. A psychiatrist. He witnessed the whole thing."

The judge nodded. "I'll allow it."

Erin put the tape in the player on the clerk's desk.

She pressed play.

*Hiss. Static.*

Then, the voice.

It wasn't Emily. It wasn't human. It was a sound from the bottom of a well, wet and grinding.

*"One... two... three... four... five... six..."*

The jurors leaned forward.

*"We are the ones who dwell within."*

A shiver went through the room. It was visceral. The temperature seemed to drop ten degrees.

"Father," Erin said, her voice steady. "Who is speaking on that tape?"

"The demon," Moore said. "Or rather, the demons. There were six of them."

"Six?"

"Yes. They named themselves. Cain. Nero. Judas. Legion. Belial. And..."

Moore paused. He looked at the crucifix on the wall behind the judge.

"And Lucifer."

On the tape, the voice laughed. It was a sound of pure malice.

*"Trick or treat,"* it hissed. *"I give you treats and tricks."*

Suddenly, the tape cut to a different sound. Screaming. Not the demon. Emily.

And then, the sound of cats. Screeching, hissing, a whirlwind of feline rage.

"What is that?" Erin asked.

"The cats," Moore said. "Barn cats. They attacked us. They swarmed into the room."

Owen Thomas scoffed. "Cats, Father? You're saying the Devil controls cats?"

"I'm saying the natural order was subverted," Moore said. "The horses. The insects. The weather. Everything was wrong that night."

Erin stopped the tape. The silence that rushed back into the room was deafening.

She looked at the jury. They weren't scoffing. They looked terrified.

She had opened the door. Now she just had to walk them through it.
