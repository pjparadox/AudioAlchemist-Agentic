## Chapter 6: Sidebar

The bar was called "Sidebar," a joke that stopped being funny three martinis in. It was a cavern of polished wood and brass, smelling of gin, expensive cologne, and the sweat of high-stakes litigation. It was where the city's legal sharks came to feed on appetizers and each other.

Erin Bruner sat at the end of the bar, an island of focus in the sea of noise. She was in her thirties, striking in a way that made men look twice and then look away, intimidated. She wore a suit that cost more than most people's cars, cut to show off a figure she didn't have time to date with. Her hair was pulled back, revealing a face that was beautiful but tired. The kind of tired that sleep doesn't fix. The kind that comes from selling your soul by the hour.

She was marking legal pleadings with a Montblanc pen, the ink flowing like black blood. *Slash. Slash. Slash.* Cutting the weak arguments from the herd.

"There's ambition," a voice drawled.

Erin didn't look up. She knew the voice. Karl Gunderson. Senior Partner. The man whose name was on the door she wanted to walk through. The man who held the keys to the kingdom.

"She never stops," Karl said to the circle of cronies surrounding him—men with silver hair and gold watches, the old guard of the firm. "Gentlemen, you know Erin Bruner."

Erin capped her pen. She turned, flashing a smile that was purely professional—a weapon she kept in her arsenal.

"Only by reputation," one of the cronies said, lifting a glass of scotch. "The Van Hopper trial. You got a murderer off, didn't you?"

"The system worked," Erin said smoothly. Her voice was cool, detached.

"Or you worked the system," the man countered, chuckling. "It's not like the jury said he was innocent."

Erin picked up her martini. The glass was cold, sweating condensation. "But then, who is?"

The men laughed. Cynicism was the currency of the Sidebar, and Erin was rich.

"Excuse us, gentlemen," Karl said, taking Erin's arm. He steered her away from the pack, toward a quieter corner where the shadows were deeper.

"Have you seen the news?" Karl asked, his voice dropping. "The priest?"

"I heard," Erin said. "Exorcism gone wrong. Negligent homicide. Sounds messy. I don't do messy."

"The D.A. charged him. They offered a plea—reckless endangerment. He turned it down."

"Smart man," Erin said. "Or a fool."

"The Archdiocese requested you," Karl said.

Erin paused, the glass halfway to her lips. "Me? Why?"

"Because they watched the Van Hopper trial. They think you're smart. They think you're convincing. They want you to get him to take the deal. Avoid a trial. Avoid the embarrassment."

"And if he won't?"

"He has to," Karl said. "The Church doesn't want a spectacle. They don't want demons on the 6 o'clock news."

Erin looked at him. She saw the calculation in his eyes. This wasn't a request; it was a test.

"What's in it for me?" she asked.

"You can't win if you don't play, Erin."

"And don't play if you can't win."

"Do this for me," Karl said, leaning closer. He smelled of Chivas and power. "Get the deal. And the rising star keeps rising. The case is yours."

"I want full partner, Karl. My name on the door. Right next to yours."

Karl laughed, a low, wet sound. "That's asking a lot."

"Like you said," Erin replied, finishing her drink. "Can't win if you don't play."

She signaled the bartender. "His tab."

She walked out, her heels clicking on the hardwood like gunshots. Karl watched her go, a mix of admiration and predation on his face.

## Chapter 7: The Deal

Erin stepped out into the night air. It was cold, biting. She hailed a cab.

The ride to her apartment was a blur of neon lights and wet pavement. She checked her BlackBerry. Messages from paralegals. Messages from clients. Nothing that mattered.

She thought about Karl. About the partnership. It was what she had worked for her entire life. Law school. The clerkship. The eighty-hour weeks. The missed birthdays. The relationships that withered on the vine.

It was all for this.

She got home. Her apartment was high above the city, a glass box in the sky. It was beautiful and sterile.

She poured a glass of wine. She stood at the window, looking down at the city.

A priest. An exorcism. A dead girl.

It sounded like a nightmare. Or a career-maker.

She slept fitfully. She dreamed of a door that wouldn't close.

The next morning, she went to the jail.

## Chapter 8: The Cage

The County Jail was a concrete box designed to crush the human spirit. It smelled of industrial disinfectant, unwashed bodies, and despair.

Erin walked down the hallway, her heels echoing on the linoleum. Prisoners catcalled from the cells—obscene offers, threats, pleas. She ignored them. She was armored in her suit, her ambition, her indifference.

The guard unlocked a cell door.

Father Richard Moore lay on his bunk, staring at the ceiling. He didn't look up when she entered.

"Good morning," Erin said. "I'm Erin Bruner."

Silence.

She unfolded the metal chair and sat down. "May I sit?"

"They brought that for my public defender," Moore said. His voice was gravel, rough from disuse. "I guess they left it when they decided I wouldn't kill myself with it."

He sat up.

He was a man carved from something harder than flesh. His eyes were dark, intense, burning with a fire that the gray walls couldn't dampen. He didn't look like a criminal. He looked like a prophet in the wilderness.

"My firm represents the Archdiocese," Erin said.

"I've been expecting them," Moore said. "Why you?"

"I wanted this case," Erin lied.

"You like the spotlight," Moore said. It wasn't a question. "You defended James Van Hopper. And now you've come to further your celebrity."

"No," Erin said, meeting his gaze. "I'm here to make senior partner."

Moore smiled slightly. It was a surprising expression, devoid of judgment. "Are you Catholic?"

"No. I'm agnostic, I guess. I'm not sure."

"If you're not sure, then you are one," Moore said. "I'm sorry, Ms. Bruner. I don't think you're the right attorney for me. I'll stick with the public defender."

"He can't get you a deal," Erin said, leaning forward. "I can. The D.A. is offering reckless endangerment. Time served. You walk out of here today."

"I won't accept a plea."

"Why? Pride? Martyrdom?"

"Truth," Moore said. "I want Emily's story to be told."

"Her story?" Erin asked. "That the ritual killed her?"

"That she died for a reason," Moore said. "I don't care about my reputation. I don't care about jail. I care about the truth. I want the world to know what happened to her. And why."

Erin stared at him. In her world, truth was negotiable. It was a construct built of evidence and argument. But Moore spoke of it as a solid thing, a rock.

"If I represent you," Moore said, "will you let me testify? Will you let me tell the truth?"

Erin calculated. A trial. A media circus. The D.A. coming for blood. It was risky. It was dangerous.

It was the only way to win.

"Fine," Erin said. "I'll let you tell her story. But only if you agree to let me do whatever I have to do to win."

Moore studied her face, looking for the cracks in her armor. "Deal."

## Chapter 9: The Girl Who Dreamed

Six months ago.

The Rose farmhouse was alive with the sound of laughter.

Emily Rose, eighteen years old, stood in her bedroom. The afternoon sun streamed through the window, catching dust motes in a golden dance. She held a letter in her hands.

Her sisters crowded around her—Alice, the youngest, with her gleaming braces; Maria, quiet and watchful; Sarah, the middle child.

"Read it!" Alice squealed, jumping on the bed. "Read it again!"

Emily's hands were shaking. "We are pleased to offer you a full scholarship..."

She looked up. Her eyes were shining. "They want me! The University! They want me!"

She grabbed Alice and spun her around. They collapsed onto the bed, a tangle of limbs and joy.

The door opened.

Maria Rose stood in the doorway. She held a dish towel in her hands. She looked at the letter. She looked at her daughter's radiant face.

She didn't smile.

"Mama!" Emily cried. "Look!"

Maria took the letter. She read it slowly, her lips moving silently.

"This is your dream, Emily," she said. Her voice was quiet.

"Yes! I'm going to be a teacher, Mama. I'm going to learn everything."

Maria nodded. She handed the letter back. She looked at Emily, and for a moment, the girl saw a flash of fear in her mother's eyes. Pure, unadulterated fear.

"You will be leaving us," Maria whispered. "To the city. We will not be there to protect you."

"I'll be okay, Mama," Emily said, hugging her. "I promise. God will be with me."

Maria held her daughter tight. "I'm going to show this to your father," she said.

She left the room.

The girls fell silent. The shadow of their mother's fear lingered in the room, cooling the sunbeams.

Then Emily smiled. She poked Alice in the ribs. "I'm going to college!"

They giggled, the shadow forgotten.

But outside, the wind picked up, rattling the windowpane.

## Chapter 10: Departure

The day Emily left, the sky was a brilliant, aching blue.

Samuel loaded her trunk into the old station wagon. He moved slowly, checking the ropes three times. He didn't want to let go.

Emily stood on the porch. She wore her best coat. She held her bag.

"Do you have your Rosary?" Maria asked.

"Yes, Mama."

"Do you have your warm socks?"

"Yes, Mama."

"Do not walk alone at night," Samuel said, closing the trunk. "The city is not like here. There are... wolves."

"I know, Papa."

Father Moore arrived to bless the car. He sprinkled holy water on the hood. He pressed a small Bible into Emily's hand.

"Remember who you are, Emily," he said. "Remember where you come from."

"I will, Father."

She got into the car. She rolled down the window.

"I love you!" she called out as they drove away.

She watched them recede in the rearview mirror. Her mother, wiping her eyes. Her father, standing like a stone. Her sisters, waving.

The farmhouse shrank. The fields blurred.

As they turned onto the main road, Emily felt a sudden chill. A shiver that started at the base of her spine and crawled up her neck.

She looked out the window.

For a second, the cornstalks didn't look like plants. They looked like grasping fingers, reaching for the car. Reaching for her.

She blinked. Just corn. Just wind.

She clutched her Bible.

*I'm going to be okay,* she told herself. *I'm going to be free.*

The car sped up, carrying her toward the city. Toward the future. Toward the dark.
