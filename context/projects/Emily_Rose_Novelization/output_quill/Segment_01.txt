The wind didn't howl; it accused. It pressed against the frosted glass of the farmhouse windows, seeking a weakness, a crack in the world to whistle through. Outside, the landscape of the Midwest lay under a shroud of pale green predawn twilight—a flat, grey expanse of harvested fields and frozen mud that stretched to the horizon like a forgotten memory.

The car that crunched up the unpaved driveway was a dark intrusion in the monochrome world. The engine ticked as it cooled, the only sound other than the whisper of empty cornstalks shivering in the bitter wind.

A man stepped out. He was in his late sixties, with a face worn smooth by years of looking at things people preferred to ignore. He was the Medical Examiner, a man of science and cold tables and fluorescent lights, but out here, standing on the frozen earth of the Rose family farm, science felt thin. It felt like a coat that was too light for the weather.

He buttoned his overcoat against the biting gust and walked to the porch. He knocked.

The sound was swallowed by the silence. It wasn't an empty silence; it was a heavy, listening silence.

He waited. He shifted his weight, his boots creaking on the wood. He blew on his hands, watching his breath plume and vanish. When no one answered, he stepped down and drifted around the side of the house.

The barn stood apart, a hulking shape against the grey sky. A weather vane atop the roof creaked, spinning lazily despite the wind, as if obeying a different set of laws. Inside, horses stamped—a nervous, rhythmic thudding against the wood. *Thud. Thud. Thud.* Like a heartbeat that was too fast.

Cats were everywhere. Feral, slinking shapes that poured around the foundation of the house like liquid shadow. They watched him with eyes that reflected no light, moving with a fluid, predatory grace that made the hair on the back of his neck stand up. They didn't meow. They just watched.

And then he saw it.

Under the eaves of the porch, pulsing like a grey, papery heart, was a hornets' nest.

It was wrong. It was deeply, fundamentally wrong. It was the dead of winter. The frost lay thick on the ground. Yet the insects swarmed around the nest, sluggish but alive, defying the cold that should have killed them months ago. They moved with a feverish, unnatural energy, a biological impossibility that made the M.E.'s stomach turn.

*The laws of physics are just suggestions here,* the house seemed to whisper. *Something is broken.*

He stared at the swarm, mesmerized by the violation of nature, until the sound of a door opening snapped him back.

He turned. Maria Rose stood in the doorway. She was a woman in her forties, but grief had aged her a decade. Her eyes were rimmed with red, her face hollowed out as if she had been weeping for days. She wiped her hands on her apron, a nervous, repetitive motion.

"You are the Medical Examiner?" she asked. Her voice was brittle, like dry leaves.

The M.E. nodded. "Yes, ma'am."

She didn't say anything else. She just held the door open and stepped back. He followed her inside, leaving the cold and the impossible hornets behind.

The kitchen was warm, but it wasn't a comforting warmth. It was stifling, thick with the smell of stale coffee and exhausted bodies. A young man sat at the table, his hands wrapped around a coffee mug as if it were the only anchor in a storm. He looked shell-shocked, his eyes staring at nothing.

Three teenage girls sat nearby, a huddled mass of flannel and tears. They wept softly, touching each other, seeking comfort in contact.

Maria led him past them, into the hallway. A large wooden crucifix hung on the wall, dominating the space. The M.E. glanced at it. The Christ figure looked pained, as always, but here, in this house, the agony seemed... active. It seemed to vibrate in the air.

In the living room, Samuel Rose sat in an easy chair. He was facing the television, but the set was off. He was staring into the grey static of the unlit screen as if watching a tragedy only he could see.

In his hands, he clutched a dirty, one-eyed baby doll. His knuckles were white.

"Mr. Rose?" the M.E. said softly.

Samuel didn't blink. He didn't move. He just sat there, holding the broken doll, lost in the wreckage of his life.

Maria touched the M.E.'s arm. "Upstairs."

They climbed the stairs. The wood groaned under their feet, a protest against the intrusion. At the top of the landing, a door stood slightly ajar. Light sliced across the hallway floor.

Before they reached it, the door opened.

Father Richard Moore stepped out.

He wasn't what the M.E. expected. He wasn't a soft, suburban priest with soft hands and platitudes. He was gaunt, intense, with eyes that looked like they had looked into a furnace and refused to blink. He wore his collar not as a uniform, but as armor. He looked like a veteran of a war that no one else knew was being fought.

The two men locked eyes. Science and Faith. The scalpel and the cross.

The priest didn't speak. He just stepped aside, his gaze heavy with a knowledge that the M.E. didn't want to share.

Maria led the M.E. into the bedroom. A uniformed policeman stood in the corner, looking uncomfortable, trying to make himself small.

The door closed.

***

Time passed. Or maybe it didn't. In the farmhouse, time felt fluid, sticky.

Later, in the kitchen, Father Moore stood by the window, staring out at the frozen fields. The only sound was the ticking of the old wooden clock on the wall. *Tick. Tick. Tick.* Measuring out the seconds of a tragedy that had already ended.

Maria, Samuel, and the young man, Jason, sat at the table. The girls were gone.

The door opened. The M.E. and the policeman entered.

The M.E. looked shaken. He looked like a man who had seen something that didn't fit in his world view, something that couldn't be categorized or autopsied.

Samuel looked up from the broken doll. His eyes were pleading. *Tell me it's not true. Tell me she's just sleeping.*

"I'm sorry, Mr. Rose," the M.E. said. His voice trembled slightly. "But I cannot state conclusively that the cause of death is natural."

He paused. He swallowed hard.

"Your daughter... she..."

He trailed off. He couldn't say it. He couldn't describe the twisted state of the body upstairs, the malnutrition, the sheer physical ruin of a nineteen-year-old girl. He couldn't describe the way her muscles had locked, or the look on her face—a rictus of terror that death hadn't erased.

The silence stretched, tight as a piano wire.

Father Moore turned back to the window. He looked out at the backyard.

The wind was kicking up again, swirling the snow into little devils. But beyond the snow, standing in the shadow of the trees, was a figure.

Tall. Draped in a long black robe. A broad-brimmed black hat obscured the face.

It wasn't a farmer. It wasn't a neighbor.

The figure stood perfectly still, the heavy fabric of the robes flaring in the wind. Watching. Waiting.

Moore felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. He knew that figure. He had seen it before.

"Excuse me... Father?" the policeman said.

Moore blinked. He looked back at the policeman, then quickly back to the yard.

The figure turned. It walked into the wind, moving with a gliding smoothness. It stepped into the deep shadow of the barn and dissolved. Gone. Like smoke.

"Father Moore," the policeman said. His hand rested on his belt, near his cuffs. His voice was apologetic but firm. "You'll have to come with me."

Moore turned slowly. He looked at the family. He looked at the cross on the wall.

"I am ready," he said.
