Chapter 7: The Burning Bush

"Dr. Sadira Adani," Erin announced.

The courtroom doors swung open. Dr. Adani walked in. She was a woman of presence, wearing a colorful shawl that seemed too bright for the drab courtroom. She carried herself with the confidence of someone who had stared into the abyss and taken notes.

"I am an anthropologist," she testified. "I study spiritual phenomena. Possession."

"Is possession real?" Erin asked.

"To the people experiencing it? Absolutely."

"And scientifically?"

"It is a trans-cultural phenomenon," Adani said. "It appears in every society in human history. The symptoms are consistent. Aversion to sacred objects. Speaking in languages unknown to the subject. Superhuman strength."

"And Emily?"

"She fit the profile perfectly. She was a 'hypersensitive.' Someone with a thin barrier between this world and the... other."

Owen Thomas stood up for cross-examination. He looked bored.

"Dr. Adani," he said. "You believe in magic?"

"I believe in human experience."

"You believe a girl can be possessed by a ghost?"

"An entity."

"Right. An entity. And this entity... it hates medicine?"

"The drug Tegretol," Adani said, her voice tightening, "dulls the brain. It locks the mind. An exorcism requires the subject to be lucid. To reject the invader. The drugs prevented that. They trapped her in the possession."

"So the medicine killed her?" Owen asked, smiling incredulously.

"The medicine prevented the cure," Adani corrected.

Owen laughed. It was a short, sharp bark. "Thank you, Doctor. You may go back to your witch doctor studies."

Adani flushed, but she held her head high as she stepped down.

Erin watched the jury. They were divided. The older ones, the churchgoers, they were nodding. The younger ones looked skeptical.

She needed to tip the scales.

"The Defense calls Dr. Edmund Cartwright."

Silence.

"Dr. Cartwright?" the bailiff called out.

The doors didn't open.

Erin felt a cold knot in her stomach. She looked at Ray, her investigator. He was on his phone, looking pale.

He signaled her. *Come here.*

Erin asked for a recess. She met Ray in the hallway.

"Where is he?" she hissed.

"He's... gone," Ray said.

"Gone? Did he run?"

"He's dead, Erin."

Erin grabbed the wall to steady herself. "What?"

"A car accident. Last night. A hit and run. He was crossing the street near the park."

Erin closed her eyes. She saw Cartwright's face in the park, sweaty, terrified. *They're watching me.*

"Did he... was it an accident?"

Ray looked at her. "He was hit by a truck. The driver didn't stop. There were no skid marks, Erin. No brake lights."

Erin felt the blood drain from her face. She felt the locket around her neck, heavy and cold.

"We have to tell the judge," she whispered.

Chapter 8: The Shadow in the Cell

Father Moore took the news with a terrifying calmness.

"He is at peace now," Moore said in his cell.

"Peace?" Erin snapped. "He's dead! He was our only corroborating witness! Without him, the tape is just... a recording of a sick girl acting crazy."

"It is not acting," Moore said. "You know that."

"I don't know anything anymore," Erin said. She sat on the bunk, putting her head in her hands. "I'm losing, Father. I'm losing your life."

"You are fighting," Moore said. "That is enough."

"It's not enough! I need proof!"

"You have the letter."

"What letter?"

Moore reached into his Bible. He pulled out a yellow envelope. It looked old, handled a thousand times.

"Emily wrote this," he said. "The morning after the exorcism failed. She gave it to me before she died. She said... she said it was the answer."

Erin took the envelope. "Why didn't you give me this before?"

"Because you weren't ready."

Erin opened it. The handwriting was shaky, spider-web thin.

*Flashback.*

The morning after Halloween.

The farmhouse was quiet. The storm had passed. The sun was rising, painting the frost-covered fields in diamonds.

Emily woke up. She was in her bed. Her body ached. Every muscle, every bone felt bruised.

She got up. She walked downstairs. The house was asleep.

She walked out the back door. The cold air hit her face. It felt clean.

She walked into the field. The mist was rising from the ground, swirling around her ankles.

She walked until the house was just a shape in the fog.

Then, she fell.

Her legs gave out. She collapsed into the wet grass.

She looked up.

The mist parted.

A figure stood there. Not the black figure. Not the man in the hat.

A woman.

She was dressed in simple robes. She glowed with a soft, golden light that didn't hurt the eyes.

"Emily," the woman said.

Emily wept. "Why?" she sobbed. "Why didn't they leave? I prayed. Father Moore prayed."

"I know," the woman said. Her voice was like water, like music. "Heaven is not blind to your pain."

"Take them away," Emily begged. "Please. I can't bear it."

"I can take you," the woman said. "I can cut the cord. You can come with me now. To peace. To rest."

Emily looked at the woman's hand. It was extended. An invitation.

"Or," the woman said, "you can stay."

"Stay?"

"You can continue this. You can suffer. You can let them break your body."

"Why?" Emily whispered. "Why would I choose that?"

"Because," the woman said, "through your suffering, others will see. They will know that we are real. That the war is real. People say God is dead. But how can they believe that if you show them the Devil?"

Emily looked at the ground. She saw the frost. She saw the beauty of the world.

She thought of Jason. Of her sisters. Of the people who lived their lives in the grey, never knowing the light or the dark.

She looked up. Her eyes were clear.

"I choose to stay," she said.

The woman smiled. It was a sad smile, but proud.

"Then go," she said. "Work out your salvation with fear and trembling."

The vision faded.

Emily was alone in the field.

She looked at her hands.

In the center of her palms, the skin split. Blood welled up. Red and bright.

The Stigmata.

Present Day.

Erin finished reading. She was crying. She didn't know when she had started.

"She chose it," Erin whispered. "She chose to die."

"She chose to witness," Moore corrected.

"And the wounds? The stigmata?"

"They were real," Moore said. "I saw them. Her parents saw them."

"We can use this," Erin said, wiping her eyes. The lawyer in her was waking up, clawing through the emotion. "It explains why she stopped the exorcisms. Why she stopped the medicine. It wasn't negligence. It was... acceptance."

"It was martyrdom," Moore said.

Erin stood up. She felt a surge of energy. "I'm putting you back on the stand."

"Karl will fire you," Moore warned.

"Let him," Erin said. She touched the locket at her throat. "I'm done playing his game."
