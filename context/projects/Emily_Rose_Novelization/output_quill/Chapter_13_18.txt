# Chapter 13: The Exorcism

Halloween.

The farmhouse was an island in a sea of darkness. The storm raged outside, rain lashing the windows like gravel. Inside, the air was thick, pressurized. It felt like being underwater.

Emily lay on the bed. She was tied down with strips of heavy cloth. Her body was rigid, vibrating with a low-frequency hum that rattled the teeth of everyone in the room.

Father Moore stood over her. He wore his stole, purple for penance. He held his Bible like a shield.

"We begin," he said.

He sprinkled holy water on her.

*Sizzle.*

It sounded like meat hitting a hot grill. Steam rose from Emily's skin.

She screamed. It was a sound that started as a girl's cry and tore itself open into a predator's roar.

"IT BURNS!"

She thrashed, the bed frame groaning. Her wrists twisted in the restraints, the skin tearing, blood weeping into the cloth.

"Exorcizamus te!" Moore shouted, his voice fighting the thunder. "Omnis immundus spiritus!"

Emily's head snapped up. Her eyes were gone. No iris, no pupil. Just a flat, matte blackness that absorbed the dim light.

"Your Latin is sloppy, Priest," she sneered. The voice was urbane, mocking. It sounded like a bored aristocrat. "You missed a declension."

In the hallway, the crucifix on the wall rattled. *Bang. Bang.* Then, slowly, deliberately, the nail gave way. It swung upside down.

In the bedroom, the shadows moved. Not cast by any light, they detached from the corners, slinking across the floor like oil.

"Who are you?" Moore demanded.

"I am the one who dwells within," the voice hissed.

Suddenly, Emily sat up. The cloth restraints didn't loosen; they *snapped*. Thick canvas tore like wet paper.

She grabbed the doll from the nightstand—the one-eyed baby doll—and hurled it. It smashed against the wall, the plastic head shattering.

Then, the door creaked open.

Eyes shone in the darkness.

Cats.

They poured through the door, a wave of fur and claws. Barn cats. Feral. They didn't meow; they shrieked. They launched themselves at Moore, a whirlwind of teeth and claws, biting at his legs, his cassock.

Moore fell, shielding his face.

In the chaos, Emily moved. She didn't walk; she flowed. She launched herself at the window.

*CRASH.*

Glass exploded outward. She was gone, diving into the storm, into the night.

# Chapter 14: The Barn

The barn was a cathedral of rot.

The rain hammered the tin roof, a deafening drumroll. Inside, the air smelled of wet hay, horse manure, and sulfur.

Samuel Rose chased his daughter. "Emily! Wait!"

He found her near the water trough. She was plunging her head into the icy water.

Steam rose from the trough. The water was boiling around her.

"Help me!" Samuel shouted.

Jason and Moore rushed in. They dragged her onto the straw. She was heavy, impossibly heavy, dense as lead.

"Hold her!" Moore yelled.

Emily opened her mouth.

She howled.

It wasn't a human sound. It was a biologically impossible noise—the shriek of a primate, the roar of a lion, the screech of a raptor—all layered on top of each other. It vibrated in the marrow of their bones.

The horses in the stalls went mad. The black stallion kicked the wood, splintering the heavy oak, eyes rolling white in terror.

Dr. Cartwright, the psychiatrist, huddled in the corner, covering his ears, sobbing. His science had broken.

"Names!" Moore roared, kneeling over her, the Bible clutched like a weapon. "I command you! Tell me your names!"

Emily stopped struggling. She went perfectly still.

She opened her mouth.

The barn filled with insects.

Or maybe it didn't. Maybe it was just in their minds. But they saw them. Roaches pouring from the cracks in the beams. Spiders dropping on silk threads like paratroopers. Snakes coiling in the hay.

Moore brushed a centipede off his bible. He didn't stop.

"TELL ME!"

Emily’s face contorted, muscles shifting under the skin like worms. She sat up, levitating inches off the straw.

"One," she whispered. The voice was ancient, dusty. "I am the one who dwelt within Cain!"

Thunder cracked. The barn shook.

"Two!" A hiss, sharp and sibilant. "I am the one who dwelt within Nero!"

"Three!" A growl. "I once dwelt within Judas!"

"Four!" A giggle, high and manic. "I was with Legion!"

"Five!" A voice of cold, heavy iron. "I am Belial!"

Moore was sweating, his face pale. The spiritual pressure was crushing him. "And the sixth? Who are you?"

Emily dropped to the ground. She looked up at him. Her eyes were black voids.

"And I..."

The shadows in the barn seemed to lengthen, to stand up. A darkness coalesced behind her, forming a shape. A cloak. A hat.

The Black Figure.

"I am Lucifer," the voice boomed, doubling on itself, a chorus of one. "The Devil in the Flesh!"

The stallion broke free. It charged, kicking Samuel in the chest, sending him flying across the barn. Then it galloped out into the night.

Emily collapsed.

The insects vanished. The figure dissolved.

Silence rushed back in, louder than the storm.

# Chapter 15: The Aftermath

The courtroom was silent. The tape ended.

Erin pressed stop.

"Six names," Father Moore said from the stand. "Cain. Nero. Judas. Legion. Belial. Lucifer."

Owen Thomas stood up. He walked to the jury box. He looked bored.

"Tibetan monks," he said.

Moore blinked. "Excuse me?"

"Tibetan monks," Owen repeated. "They can create multiphonics. Two tones at once. Did you know that?"

"No."

"So the 'demonic voices' on the tape... the 'chorus from hell'... could simply be a psychotic girl straining her vocal cords?"

"It wasn't a trick," Moore said quietly.

"And the names?" Owen asked. "Cain? Nero? Judas? All figures from history. Figures a girl interested in religion would know."

"She didn't know Aramaic," Moore said. "Or Latin."

"She studied catechism, didn't she? She was a good Catholic girl. She took electives."

Owen leaned on the railing. "And this 'Black Figure' you saw? Did anyone else see it?"

"No," Moore admitted.

"Just you. The man who stopped her medication."

Owen smiled. It was a predator's smile.

"No further questions."

# Chapter 16: The Death of a Witness

Erin sat at the defense table. She felt the weight of the room pressing down on her. The tape had terrified them, but Owen was giving them a lifeboat: Logic. Science. Monks.

She needed Cartwright.

She found him in the parking lot behind the courthouse. It was dusk. The streetlights buzzed, flickering in the cold wind.

Cartwright was leaning against his car. He looked worse than he had in the park. His eyes were wide, the pupils dilated. He smelled of scotch and fear.

"Dr. Cartwright!" Erin called.

He spun around. He looked past her, at the tree line.

"I can't do it," he stammered. "I can't go back in there."

"You have to," Erin said, grabbing his arm. "You're the only one who can corroborate the tape. You were there."

Cartwright pulled away. "They're watching, Erin. Don't you see them?"

"Who?"

"The dark ones."

He stepped back. "Tell Moore I'm sorry. Tell him... I know."

He stepped into the road.

The truck came out of nowhere. No headlights. Just a roar of an engine and the squeal of tires.

*CRUNCH.*

It hit him with the force of a battering ram. Cartwright flew over the hood. He hit the windshield, glass spider-webbing around his impact, then rolled off onto the asphalt.

The truck skidded, then sped away.

Erin ran to him.

Cartwright lay twisted. His neck was broken, his head turned 180 degrees, staring back at his own spine. His eyes were open, fixed on the sky. Fixed on something Erin couldn't see.

The blood didn't pool; it spread, dark and thick, steaming in the cold air. The silence that followed was absolute.

# Chapter 17: The Crisis

Erin sat in her office. It was dark. She poured scotch into a coffee mug.

Karl walked in. He threw a newspaper on her desk.

**FATHER MOORE SMELLED THE DEVIL.**

"What were you thinking?" Karl shouted. "You put him on the stand? You let him talk about ghosts?"

"I had a witness," Erin said, her voice dull. "Cartwright."

"Cartwright is dead!" Karl yelled. "And you're finished. If you put that priest on the stand again, I will fire you."

He stormed out.

Erin put her head on the desk and wept.

She went to the jail.

"We've lost," she told Moore. "Cartwright is dead."

Moore nodded slowly. He didn't look surprised. "Then it falls to you, Erin."

"To me? I'm a lawyer, not a priest. I'm broken, Father. I'm drunk."

"You are fighting," Moore said. "That is enough."

"It's not enough! I need proof!"

"You have the letter."

"What letter?"

Moore reached into his Bible. He pulled out a yellow envelope. It looked old, handled a thousand times.

"Emily wrote this," he said. "The morning after the exorcism failed. She gave it to me before she died. She said... she said it was the answer."

Erin took the envelope. "Why didn't you give me this before?"

"Because you weren't ready."

# Chapter 18: The Vision

Erin went home. She sat on her sofa. She opened the letter.

*Flashback.*

The morning after Halloween.

The farmhouse was quiet. The storm had passed. The sun was rising, painting the frost-covered fields in diamonds.

Emily woke up. She was in her bed. Her body ached. Every muscle, every bone felt bruised.

She got up. She walked downstairs. The house was asleep.

She walked out the back door. The cold air hit her face. It felt clean.

She walked into the field. The mist was rising from the ground, swirling around her ankles.

She walked until the house was just a shape in the fog.

Then, she fell.

Her legs gave out. She collapsed into the wet grass.

She looked up.

The mist parted.

A figure stood there. Not the black figure. Not the man in the hat.

A woman.

She was dressed in simple robes. She glowed with a soft, golden light that didn't hurt the eyes.

"Emily," the woman said.

Emily wept. "Why?" she sobbed. "Why won't they leave? I prayed. Father Moore prayed."

"I know," the woman said. Her voice was like water, like music. "Heaven is not blind to your pain."

"Take them away," Emily begged. "Please. I can't bear it."

"I can take you," the woman said. "I can cut the cord. You can come with me now. To peace. To rest."

Emily looked at the woman's hand. It was extended. An invitation.

"Or," the woman said, "you can stay."

"Stay?"

"You can continue this. You can suffer. You can let them break your body."

"Why?" Emily whispered. "Why would I choose that?"

"Because," the woman said, "through your suffering, others will see. They will know that we are real. That the war is real. People say God is dead. But how can they believe that if you show them the Devil?"

Emily looked at the ground. She saw the frost. She saw the beauty of the world.

She thought of Jason. Of her sisters. Of the people who lived their lives in the grey, never knowing the light or the dark.

She looked up. Her eyes were clear.

"I choose to stay," she said.

The woman smiled. It was a sad smile, but proud.

"Then go," she said. "Work out your salvation with fear and trembling."

The vision faded.

Emily was alone in the field.

She looked at her hands.

In the center of her palms, the skin split. Blood welled up. Red and bright.

The Stigmata.
